'He never needs to drop again': The legacy of Frank Ocean's Blonde

10 years ago, the Grammy award-winning artist dropped Blonde, and music changed forever. Here, fans reflect on what the album has meant and continues to mean to them

10 years ago, Christopher Edwin Breaux, known to the world as Frank Ocean, dropped his second studio album, Blonde. Released 48 hours after publishing his ambient 45-minute visual album Endless (the album he used to fulfil his contract obligations to Def Jam and Universal), the four-year-long drought, filled with cryptic Tumblr posts, letters and a library due date card with random dates, finally came to a disorienting and abundant end: two albums and a glossy accompanying magazine, Boys Don’t Cry.

Endless set the internet ablaze. Launched on a live stream, where dedicated, hungry fans watched Ocean build boxes and watch paint dry over previews of instrumental music. Pitchfork's Ryan Dombal called the whole spectacle “painfully dull”. But that all got overshadowed when Blonde debuted.

“R.I.P. Trayvon, that nigga look just like me”, Ocean's autotuned child-like voice sings on lead single “Nikes”. The four years between Ocean’s critically acclaimed debut album Channel Orange and Blonde saw a modern civil rights movement erupt in the US, prompted by the brutal murders of 12-year-old Trayvon Martin; Michael Brown, Eric Garner, Tamir Rice, Eric Harris, Walter Scott, Freddie Grey, Alton Sterling, and many more by the police (or in the case of Martin, a neighbourhood watch volunteer, who was aquitted of all charges and served no prison time). Blonde is not a particularly political album, but this line is both its most overly political moment and one of its most memorable – a simple, haunting recognition that Martin’s unjust end could happen to any young Black man.

It’s a powerful introduction to an album that explores many themes: masculinity, drugs, family, queerness, youth, morality, self-love and self-hatred, and falling in and out of love. For many young people, it was the album they came of age to; an album so emotionally intelligent that listening to it felt like waking up for the first time. “To put it simply, Blonde gave boys my age access to a musical and emotional vocabulary that hadn't existed before,” 29-year-old Jacob tells Dazed.

Blonde, in all its minimal simplicity (it mostly uses guitar and keyboard loops, albeit punctured by moments of psychedelic splendor, drawing influence from the likes of the Beach Boys and Todd Rundgren along with the R&B greats), is surprisingly difficult to describe. One thing's for sure: it is profoundly affecting. Listening to it 10 years on, it’s difficult not to think of Ocean’s brother, Ryan Breaux, who died in a car crash in 2020 at the age of 18, and who features on the album’s final song “Futura Free”. Lines like “we’ll never be those kids again” on “Ivy,” which were always true, painful reflections on the passage of time, now hurt in new and unexpected ways, all these years later.
